Safari, Edge and Chrome are adding Global Privacy Control. It's a good switch. It just doesn't do what the headlines promise.

Global Privacy Control, GPC for short, is one setting in your browser. When it's on, every page you open gets a short note: don't sell or share my data. Technically it's a header, Sec-GPC: 1, plus a flag that page scripts can read.

In California and a growing list of US states, sites must treat that note as an opt-out. In Europe, a site needs your consent before it tracks you, and no law says a browser signal counts as your answer. So the banner stays.

Turn on both.

The switch tells sites not to sell your data. BannerBye tells them no to everything else, before the banner shows: it sends the same GPC signal, answers the consent framework most European publishers use with a refusal, and clicks the reject button where there is one.

Europe tried to make browser signals binding. The European Commission's proposal from November 2025 included it; EU member states took it out of their position in 2026, and the European Parliament is expected to vote in 2027. Questions

Does Global Privacy Control remove cookie banners?

Not in Europe. GPC asks websites not to sell or share your data, and several US states require sites to honour it.

EU law has no rule that makes a site treat a browser signal as a refusal, so European sites keep showing their banner. BannerBye refuses that banner for you.

Should I turn on Safari's Global Privacy Control if I use BannerBye?

Yes. It costs nothing and it is your browser's own signal.

BannerBye already sends the same signal on every site, and then does the part the switch can't: it answers the banner.

Will the EU make websites respect browser signals?

Maybe, but not soon. The European Commission proposed it in November 2025. EU member states removed it from their negotiating position in 2026, and the European Parliament has not voted yet.

Even once new cookie rules are adopted, member states get 24 months to put them into national law.
